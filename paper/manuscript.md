# Who Pays for Pharmaceutical Innovation? A Reproducible Multidimensional Free-Rider Index Across High-Income Countries

# Abstract

**Objectives:** Claims that other countries "free-ride" on pharmaceutical innovation now shape US most-favored-nation pricing policy, yet existing measures rank countries on one dimension, price. We developed a multidimensional index, tested its robustness, and drew policy implications.

**Methods:** Following Basu's dynamic-efficiency model, in which a smaller market can free-ride on research and development (R&D) induced by a larger market, we combined five published indicators for nine high-income countries (G7 excluding the United States, Switzerland, Australia, South Korea), the United States serving as reference: brand-name drug price level, innovative-drug revenue share relative to gross domestic product (GDP) share, share of US-first new drugs publicly reimbursed and mean interval to reimbursement, and pharmaceutical R&D as a percentage of GDP. Components were min-max normalized and equally weighted; robustness was assessed with 21 alternative specifications, 10,000 Monte Carlo specifications with calibrated noise and alternative sources, principal component analysis, and comparison with single-metric measures.

**Results:** Scores ranged from 27 (Switzerland) to 97 (South Korea). South Korea and Australia ranked in the top three in 99% and 83% of specifications and Germany in the bottom three in 88%; Canada, France, Japan, the United Kingdom, and Italy occupied a contested middle. The payment components tracked a price-based "Freeriding Index" (Spearman ρ = 0.89), whereas the adoption components showed no rank correlation with it (ρ = -0.04).

**Conclusions:** Payment, adoption, and R&D profiles diverge, and price-only comparisons conflate them. The index describes relative position, not deviation from an efficient contribution; reconciling individually rational under-contribution with collectively efficient innovation is the policy question it frames.

**Keywords:** pharmaceutical pricing; dynamic efficiency; composite indicator; international comparison; most-favored-nation; health technology assessment

# Highlights

- Existing measures of international "free-riding" on pharmaceutical innovation are one-dimensional, usually a price ratio. We built a five-component index (payment, adoption, R&D) for nine high-income countries with calibrated uncertainty analysis and open code.
- South Korea and Australia rank highest and Germany lowest in most of 10,000 specifications; the middle is contested. Price-based measures capture the payment channel but not adoption or R&D, on which countries differ most.
- The index measures relative position, not deviation from an efficient contribution. Because R&D responds to expected revenue in every market while funding is concentrated in one national market, the results frame a collective-action problem for payers and HTA bodies outside the United States.

# Introduction

Pharmaceutical innovation is financed largely through prices paid during market exclusivity, and expected revenue is a well-documented driver of R&D effort.[1-5] Because the resulting knowledge is a global public good, countries that pay less than others benefit from research they did not fund. Basu formalized this intuition in a dynamic-efficiency model: when a large and a small market coexist, the small market can deviate from the dynamic-efficient payment rule because R&D is sustained by the larger market's payments.[6] Public-goods theory predicts this outcome as an equilibrium: smaller contributors under-provide and the largest is "exploited".[7,8] The argument has moved from theory to policy. The May 2025 US executive order on most-favored-nation (MFN) drug pricing framed foreign prices as "freeloading"[9] and is expected to raise prices in reference countries,[10] the United Kingdom agreed in December 2025 to raise net prices for new medicines and its cost-effectiveness threshold,[11,12] and the US Trade Representative opened a Section 301 investigation into Germany's "persistent underpayment" for innovative medicines in June 2026.[13]

Measurement has not kept pace. Recent quantifications each capture one dimension. Frech et al estimated each country's contribution to the global public good as price minus marginal cost times sales and found that country size largely determines it.[8] The US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) compared countries' shares of innovative-drug revenue with their shares of GDP and population.[14] Kolchinsky and Xie published a "Freeriding Index" based on net brand prices relative to income.[15] Philipson et al documented a timing channel: countries that reimburse US-first drugs years later enter after competition has eroded prices.[16] Each ranks countries differently, and none captures contribution through domestic R&D, fast reimbursement, or breadth of medicines made available.

Composite indicators summarize such multidimensional phenomena, but are credible only when their sensitivity to normalization, weighting, and aggregation is reported.[17-20] We therefore constructed a Free-Rider Index that (1) operationalizes the small-country logic with five published indicators spanning payment, adoption, and R&D input; (2) reports rank uncertainty across thousands of specifications rather than a single ranking; (3) decomposes its relationship with existing single-metric measures; and (4) releases code, data, and an interactive dashboard so that decision makers can apply their own weights. The term "free-riding" is adopted from the literature under review.[6,8,15] Three levels of claim should be kept apart: that countries differ on measurable payment, adoption, and R&D characteristics (shown directly); that these characteristics bear on global innovation incentives (theory and prior evidence); and that a particular country contributes too little (not established here).

# Methods

## Conceptual framework

In Basu's model a social payer maximizes a weighted sum of consumer and producer surplus, with weight λ on consumers, and innovators respond to the share β of surplus they can appropriate during exclusivity. The optimal share is β* = λ/[(2λ−1)(1+ηε)], where η and ε describe the innovation production function; full transfer (β* = 1) is optimal whenever λ ≤ 0.5, and with the estimated technology β* falls to 0.26 when only consumer surplus counts.[6] In a two-country simulation, requiring the small country to pay the full share raises global output from 46 to 49 new drugs a year and benefits the large country, but lowers the small country's own long-run consumer surplus by 20%, so free-riding is individually rational for it.[6] Empirical estimates of the share manufacturers capture cluster far below full transfer, at roughly 5%-15%,[21-23] and a consumer-surplus-maximizing planner would choose about 22% under push incentives.[24] β is not observed for any country; our indicators are observable correlates of the channels through which β operates, not estimates of it, and the index is a relative scale across the sampled countries, not a distance from an efficient benchmark. Two domains map onto the expected-revenue mechanism in Basu's model: a country can pay less for on-patent medicines or generate less innovative-drug revenue relative to its economic capacity (payment), or reimburse fewer new medicines and later, reducing the present value of innovators' returns (adoption). A third domain, domestic pharmaceutical R&D, is our extension: in the model, market rewards are the cause and industry R&D the response, so domestic R&D is neither part of β nor a payer's transfer. We include it because contribution to the global stock of innovation runs through research capacity as well as payment, and because omitting it would score research-intensive but low-priced countries purely as free-riders; the cost is that the composite mixes a payment measure with an activity measure, so we report the four-component payment-and-adoption index alongside the headline and read R&D as where research is performed, not who funds it. Two caveats follow. Lower prices in lower-income or lower-threshold markets can be efficient under Ramsey or value-based differential pricing,[25,26] and paying up to the health-opportunity-cost threshold is not under-payment relative to domestic value,[27] so the payment components measure relative payment, not under-payment. Launch and reimbursement timing reflect manufacturers' sequencing and regulatory review as well as payer behavior,[28-30] so the adoption components measure outcomes, not intent. Equal weights are not neutral: two payment, two adoption, and one R&D indicator give the domains 40%, 40%, and 20%, so equal domain weights are the first sensitivity analysis. Indicators were drawn from sources published or updated in 2024-2026 that cover all ten countries with a common definition.

## Countries and data

The sample comprises the G7 countries plus Switzerland, Australia, and South Korea, which account for 91% of OECD innovative-drug revenue[14] and overlap with MFN reference baskets.[10] Because the United States defines the reference values for price, availability, and interval, it serves as numeraire and is not ranked; the headline covers the nine other countries, with a ten-country variant as a sensitivity analysis. Table 1 lists the five components. Price level (P) is each country's brand-name originator price as a percentage of the US price in 2022, from RAND's bilateral, US-volume-weighted comparison of IQVIA MIDAS manufacturer prices;[31] the all-drug index, which mixes generics on which the United States is cheaper, is a sensitivity analysis. Revenue contribution (R) is a country's share of OECD revenue from innovative branded products divided by its share of OECD GDP, aggregated over 2020-2025, from the HHS Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE).[14] Availability (A) is the share of 225 novel drugs first launched in the United States in 2014-2019 that were publicly reimbursed by 2023, and interval (D) the mean years from US launch to public reimbursement among reimbursed drugs, both from Philipson et al's analysis of the industry-compiled PhRMA launch dataset (no microdata released);[16] D is conditional on reimbursement and includes sponsor filing, regulatory review, and payer decision (operational definitions in Appendix Table S8). R&D input (I) is business enterprise R&D in the pharmaceutical industry (ISIC Rev. 4 division 21) as a percentage of GDP, from the OECD for eight countries,[32] the Office for National Statistics product-group series for the United Kingdom, whose OECD value is not comparable,[33] and the Australian Bureau of Statistics line for basic chemical and chemical product manufacturing (ANZSIC 18, an upper bound) for Australia.[34] Government health-R&D budgets are not comparable where universities are block-funded (Appendix Table S1); an industry-association series[35] and industry-sponsored trial starts per million population (ClinicalTrials.gov) are alternatives. GDP and population are from the World Bank.[36] Every transcribed value and its source are listed in the repository; only public aggregate data were used, so ethics review was not required.

## Index construction

Following the OECD/JRC handbook,[17] each component was oriented so that higher values indicate more apparent free-riding (P, R, A, and I inverted), then min-max normalized to 0-100 across the nine countries; the headline index is their equal-weight arithmetic mean. Because nominal weights do not equal importance,[20] we report each component's effective importance, its squared correlation with the composite.[37]

## Robustness

We examined 21 alternative specifications (Table 2): domain weights; single-domain and four-component indices; geometric aggregation; z-score, rank, and log min-max normalization; the all-drug price index; income- and ability-to-pay-adjusted payment (income elasticity 1.3);[25,26] post-2020 revenue; an interval assigning non-reimbursed drugs the 6.5-year follow-up horizon; EFPIA W.A.I.T. adoption data for the five European countries;[38] alternative R&D indicators (association series, trial hosting, tax subsidy,[39] omission); residualization on log GDP;[7,8] and inclusion of the United States. We also dropped each component and each country in turn. Following Saisana et al,[18] we then ran 10,000 Monte Carlo specifications that jointly drew weights from a flat Dirichlet distribution, a normalization method, an aggregation rule, calibrated input noise (Table 2), and, for P, R, and I, a randomly chosen data source; we report median ranks, 5th-95th percentiles, and shares of specifications in the top and bottom three, which are frequencies under this prior rather than probabilities, and we repeat the exercise varying one source of uncertainty at a time (weights; normalization and aggregation; measurement noise; data source). A uniform gross-to-net adjustment of US prices or revenue cannot change the nine-country ranking, because min-max normalization is invariant to common scaling, and is therefore not a sensitivity analysis. Internal coherence was assessed with Spearman correlations among components and with log GDP, and with principal component analysis (PCA). Finally, we correlated the headline index and its payment and adoption sub-indices with the Kolchinsky-Xie Freeriding Index (seven overlapping countries),[15] ASPE's revenue-to-GDP and revenue-to-population ratios,[14] and Frech et al's per-capita contribution (six countries),[8] with Fisher intervals and permutation p values, since such correlations are imprecise at these sample sizes.

## Software and transparency

Analyses used Python 3.10 (pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib). Code was drafted with a large language model (Claude Fable 5.1, Anthropic, via Claude Code, September 2026) under the first author's specification; the authors reviewed, executed, and checked every function against hand calculations, a second author independently verified every transcribed value against its source, and all reported numbers are produced by the released scripts, consistent with ELEVATE-GenAI guidance.[40] No AI tool generated or altered data. Code, data, tables, figures, and a self-contained interactive dashboard are openly available [repository address withheld for anonymized review; MIT license for code, CC BY 4.0 for documentation]. Patients were not involved; sex and gender dimensions cannot be analyzed in country-level aggregates.

# Results

## Headline index

Brand-name prices ranged from 14% (South Korea) to 31% (Canada) of US levels (Table 1); the revenue-to-GDP-share ratio from 0.21 (South Korea) to 0.76 (Italy), against 1.94 for the United States; the share of US-first drugs reimbursed by 2023 from 36% (Canada, South Korea) to 73% (Germany); the mean interval from 1.1 years (Germany) to 3.2 years (South Korea); and pharmaceutical R&D from 0.02% (Canada) to 0.66% (Switzerland) of GDP.

Under equal weights (Figure 1A) the index ranged from 27.4 for Switzerland to 97.4 for South Korea. South Korea scored highest on every component except R&D, on which it ranked fifth. Australia (74.8) combined the second-lowest brand price and revenue ratio with slow, partial reimbursement and low R&D. Canada (59.9) had the highest brand price and second-highest revenue ratio among the nine but reimbursed the fewest US-first drugs, after the second-longest interval, with the lowest R&D intensity. France (48.7), Japan (42.7), the United Kingdom (40.2), and Italy (35.8) followed. Germany (28.5) and Switzerland (27.4) scored lowest: Germany because it reimburses the largest share of US-first drugs after the shortest interval, Switzerland because its R&D intensity is the highest in the sample. Scoring the United States as a tenth unit compressed the nine into 59.7-97.4 (United States 6.5) without changing their order except for Japan and the United Kingdom.

## Robustness

Across the 21 alternative specifications and five component-omission indices (Figure 2), South Korea ranked first and Australia second or third in every case. Canada ranked third in most specifications but ninth on payment components alone. Germany ranked eighth or ninth except in payment-only and price-only indices (fifth) and rank normalization (seventh); Switzerland except on adoption components alone (fourth) or income-adjusted prices (fifth), because its high income makes its brand prices look low relative to capacity. The four-component payment-and-adoption index ranked the countries in the same order except that Switzerland moved from ninth to seventh and Germany to ninth (ρ = 0.95). Spearman correlations with the headline were 0.95 or higher for every change in normalization, data source, or sample other than geometric aggregation (0.87), income-adjusted payment (0.82), and the entry of trial hosting or tax subsidies (0.87-0.92), and 0.53-0.72 for single-domain indices (Table 2). Substituting trial hosting for R&D input moved Japan, which hosts the fewest industry-sponsored trials per capita (3.4 starts per million per year, against 21.5 in Australia), from fifth to third. Dropping each country in turn left the top two and bottom two unchanged and reordered the middle (Appendix Table S10).

The Monte Carlo analysis (Figure 1B, Table 2) confirmed a stable frame around a contested middle. South Korea and Australia ranked in the top three in 99% and 83% of specifications; Germany ranked in the bottom three in 88% and Switzerland in 65%. Canada was in the top three in 58% and Japan and Italy in the bottom three in 48% and 45%, with 5th-95th percentile rank intervals spanning six or seven positions. Varying one source of uncertainty at a time (Appendix Table S9b), the middle ranks moved mainly with weights and data source, whereas measurement noise and the normalization and aggregation rules left the top and bottom three nearly fixed.

## Internal structure

The five components were only moderately correlated among the nine countries (Spearman ρ from -0.25 to 0.73). Price and revenue contribution were correlated (0.63), as were availability and interval (0.73), but the payment and adoption domains were weakly related to each other (0.17-0.35) and R&D input was uncorrelated with price (0.02) and negatively correlated with revenue contribution (-0.25). The first principal component explained 53% of the variance and the second 22%, with the second separating payment from adoption; when the United States was included, the first component explained 76%, a one-dimensionality produced by the outlier. Effective importance was 0.65-0.66 for the adoption components, 0.51-0.55 for the payment components, and 0.24 for R&D input. Availability and interval were negatively associated with country size (ρ = -0.63 and -0.50 with log GDP), whereas brand price was not (0.23); residualizing all components on log GDP left the ranking unchanged.

## Comparison with single-metric measures

The headline index was weakly and imprecisely correlated with the Kolchinsky-Xie Freeriding Index across seven overlapping countries (ρ = 0.25; Fisher 95% interval -0.62 to 0.84). Decomposition explains why (Figure 3): the payment sub-index tracked the price-based measure closely (ρ = 0.89; interval 0.43 to 0.98), as expected given their shared inputs, whereas no rank correlation was observed for the adoption sub-index (ρ = -0.04; interval -0.77 to 0.74). The measures agree on Australia but disagree on Germany, which the price-based measure ranks near the top and our index lowest, and on Canada, which it ranks lowest and our index third. Correlations with ASPE's revenue-to-GDP and revenue-to-population ratios were 0.35 and 0.60 (nine countries), and with Frech et al's per-capita contribution -0.26 (six countries; Appendix Table S6), because those size-driven measures place Japan and Italy among the larger contributors whereas adoption and R&D indicators place them mid-table.

# Discussion

This study translates the channels through which, in Basu's model, a country can rely on a larger market's R&D[6] into a measurable, adjustable index of relative position. Three findings hold across the specifications examined. First, South Korea and Australia combine low brand prices and revenue contributions with slow, partial reimbursement, whereas Germany and Switzerland contribute most, through the broadest and fastest reimbursement and through research intensity respectively. Second, the middle is contested: Canada pays the most among non-US countries for brand-name medicines but reimburses the fewest US-first drugs after the second-longest interval; Japan pays mid-range prices but hosts the fewest trials; France, the United Kingdom, and Italy cannot be ordered. Third, the payment and adoption channels are nearly orthogonal across countries, so price-based and adoption-based measures rank the same countries differently.

## Why multidimensional measurement matters

The decomposition in Figure 3 is the study's most policy-relevant result. The Kolchinsky-Xie measure, which informed the "freeloading" framing of MFN policy, is a net brand-price calculation adjusted for income.[15] Our payment sub-index tracks it closely, as it should given the shared inputs; the adoption sub-index carries information the price measure does not contain. Germany ranks near the top of the price-based measure because German prices are low relative to German income, and lowest on our index among the seven because it makes 73% of US-first drugs reimbursable, the largest share in the sample, after a mean interval of 1.1 years, and contributes 4.3% of OECD innovative-drug revenue. Canada is the mirror image: highest on brand price among the nine, lowest on reimbursement of US-first drugs (36%) after a mean interval of more than three years.[16] For innovators, non-reimbursement and delay reduce the expected return on which the dynamic-efficiency condition depends, and late adopters enter after US net prices have fallen.[16] Whether delay is the payer's doing is a separate question: manufacturers file late or not at all in low-price and heavily referenced markets,[28-30,41,42] regulatory review adds months,[43] and in Japan listing follows approval within 60 to 90 days, so the interval there reflects sponsors' filing decisions. The adoption components describe the joint outcome of payer, regulator, and firm, not HTA stringency alone. Revenue-share measures are complementary: they ask how much money flows from a country, largely a function of size,[8,14] whereas our index asks how a country compares with peers on each channel.

## Dynamic efficiency as a shared responsibility

Basu's two-country simulation is explicit about incentives: when the small country adopts the full-transfer rule, global output rises by about three new drugs a year and the large country gains, but the small country's own long-run consumer surplus falls by a fifth.[6] In the Olson-Zeckhauser equilibrium that Frech et al apply to pharmaceuticals, the public good is under-supplied and the largest contributor exploited.[7,8] The index cannot show that any country pays too little, and Basu's model does not show that a small country gains from paying more. The case for raising contributions outside the United States rests on three considerations outside that model.

First, the model holds the large country's rule fixed, and that assumption is now in doubt: under MFN, Medicare savings would exceed manufacturers' entire annual sales in the binding reference country for 73% of drugs,[44] a single international price at constant profits would raise prices outside the United States by 28% to more than 300%,[45] and European reference pricing produced convergence and delayed access.[46] If US willingness to pay falls, the small country's calculus changes, because the innovation it relied on is no longer financed. Second, the model assumes that approved drugs reach every market; in practice lower expected prices delay or foreclose launches,[28-30,41,42] Basu himself predicts that MFN referencing will lead manufacturers to delay or forgo launches in referenced markets,[6] early data show launches falling there,[47,48] and Japan's drug loss (52% of US-approved drugs from emerging biopharmaceutical companies, against 19% in Europe[49]) shows the mechanism at work. Third, R&D responds to expected revenue in every market: a 1% increase in potential market size is associated with 4%-6% more new drug entry,[1] the elasticity of new chemical entities to global patent-life revenue is about 0.25,[2] Medicare Part D raised pipeline entry,[3,50] low-reimbursement US Medicaid showed no response,[51] and China's reimbursement expansion was followed by an 86% relative rise in domestic trials.[52] Long-run elasticities of 0.25-1.5 have been reviewed.[53] As an order of magnitude, raising the nine countries' innovative-drug revenue to parity with their GDP shares would add about 16% to OECD innovative-drug revenue, implying roughly 4%-24% more new drugs per year at those elasticities; this is a long-run scenario under strong assumptions (GDP proportionality as benchmark, unchanged volumes and prices elsewhere, unchanged value of what is developed), detailed in Appendix Table S11.

Contribution need not mean paying US prices: Germany and Switzerland reach low scores through fast listing and domestic R&D, and Australia's trial hosting is a further channel. Evidence that demand pull disproportionately funds incremental products,[50,54] that opportunity-cost thresholds lie well below prevailing prices,[27,55] and that industry profitability does not by itself justify prices[56,57] argues for value-based contributions paired with transparent HTA and push instruments, not unconditional price increases. The problem the index frames is one of collective action: each country's individually rational strategy leaves global innovation under-financed and exposed to a single national market, and coordination, through negotiated contributions, joint procurement, or push funding rather than unilateral referencing, is the instrument theory suggests.[8,45,58]

## Relevance to current policy

By September 2026, 26 manufacturers had signed MFN agreements covering most of the US branded market,[59] the United Kingdom had agreed to raise net prices for new medicines by 25% and its cost-effectiveness threshold to £25,000-35,000 per quality-adjusted life-year in exchange for tariff relief,[11,12] and Germany faced a Section 301 investigation.[13] A transparent index locates each system on each channel (the UK threshold increase addresses price, not the interval to reimbursement[12,60]), gives governments a defensible alternative to price-only benchmarks with explicit weights, and provides a baseline for tracking post-MFN change.

Japan illustrates the point: mid-range brand price (22% of US) and revenue ratio (0.73), yet only 56% of US-first drugs reimbursed by 2023 and the fewest industry-sponsored trials per capita. As of March 2023, 86 of 143 medicines approved in the United States or Europe in 2016-2020 but not in Japan had no Japanese development under way,[61,62] which the health ministry attributes to pricing predictability, regulatory requirements, and the absence of emerging-company subsidiaries.[49,61] Drug loss enters the index through adoption, not payment.

## Limitations

Several limitations warrant caution. First, the index is relative: some country is always scored as the heaviest free-rider even if all contribute adequately, and the index cannot say how positions would change if US willingness to pay fell or how far one country's under-contribution is offset by others; it operationalizes the channels in Basu's model but does not test his two-country model. Second, prices are manufacturer list prices. A uniform US gross-to-net adjustment, such as RAND's 37.7% reduction,[31] leaves the nine-country ranking unchanged by construction; what would move countries is the difference in confidential rebates among comparator systems, which could not be quantified. Third, the adoption components derive from one industry-compiled dataset restricted to US-first launches;[16] they may understate adoption where drugs launch first in Europe, count products rather than clinical value, so that declining low-benefit products is scored as free-riding,[63-65] exclude early-access schemes, and reflect firms' launch strategies and regulatory timing as well as payer decisions. The European W.A.I.T. indicator is broadly concordant (rank correlation 0.70 for availability and median time; Appendix Table S7), and substituting it left the ranking unchanged. Fourth, the interval is conditional on reimbursement and follow-up differs by launch year; imputing the horizon to non-reimbursed drugs changed no rank (S13), but only drug-level data would resolve this. Fifth, pharmaceutical R&D statistics are not harmonized: OECD values for France and Italy are about one third of association figures, the United Kingdom and Australia required national series, and trial R&D by contract research organizations is not captured; the component determines Switzerland's position, and differences between series are definitional, so the data-source draws in the Monte Carlo analysis are scenario comparisons rather than sampling uncertainty. Sixth, the France-Japan-United Kingdom-Italy block reorders when individual countries are removed. Seventh, the index is a cross-section with reference years from 2014-2019 (adoption) to 2025 (revenue); it cannot capture MFN-induced price increases, and future adoption scores will partly reflect firms' MFN-driven launch delays.[30,42] Eighth, cost-effectiveness thresholds are described but not scored (Appendix Table S5). Finally, with nine countries no inferential claims are possible; disease-area heterogeneity, patient affordability, and public research funding are not captured; and "free-riding" is a descriptive label for relative position, not a judgment about fair contribution, which depends on ability to pay, system design, and the value medicines deliver.[25-27,55]

## Future work

Priorities are fixed reference bounds so that annual updates track change within countries rather than re-normalizing across them; decomposing the interval into sponsor filing, regulatory review, and payer decision; a present-value measure of expected revenue combining price, volume, adoption timing, and remaining exclusivity, which would bring the index closer to Basu's β; value-weighted availability; and extension to the full MFN reference basket.

# Conclusions

A five-component Free-Rider Index built from published data identifies a stable frame among nine high-income countries: South Korea and Australia combine low payment with slow, partial adoption; Germany and Switzerland contribute through fast listing and research intensity; the remaining five occupy a middle whose order depends on which channel is weighted. Because the payment and adoption channels are nearly independent, debates that rely on price-only measures misidentify which channel differs and which lever matters. The index describes relative position, not deviation from an efficient contribution. Basu's model shows why under-contribution is individually rational for smaller markets; the concentration of funding in one national market, the launch consequences of low expected returns, and the responsiveness of R&D to revenue in every market show why it is collectively costly. Reconciling the two is a problem of coordination, and the index is a transparent starting point for it.
