# Who Pays for Pharmaceutical Innovation? A Reproducible Multidimensional Free-Rider Index for Ten High-Income Countries

# Abstract

**Objectives:** Claims that other countries "free-ride" on pharmaceutical innovation now shape US most-favored-nation pricing policy, yet existing measures rank countries on one dimension, usually price. We developed a multidimensional index for ten high-income countries and tested robustness.

**Methods:** Following Basu's dynamic-efficiency model, in which a smaller market can free-ride on research and development (R&D) induced by a larger market, we combined five published indicators for the G7 countries, Switzerland, Australia, and South Korea: relative drug price level (2022), innovative-drug revenue share relative to gross domestic product (GDP) share (2020-2025), share of US-first new drugs reimbursed and their mean reimbursement delay, and pharmaceutical R&D as a percentage of GDP. Components were min-max normalized and equally weighted; robustness was assessed with 10,000 Monte Carlo specifications over weights, normalization, aggregation, and input noise, a jackknife, principal component analysis, and comparison with three single-metric measures.

**Results:** Scores (0-100) ranged from 6.5 (United States) to 97.4 (South Korea). South Korea, Australia, and Canada ranked in the top three in 81%-100% of specifications; Germany and Switzerland ranked in the bottom three among non-US countries in 60%-79%; France, Italy, the United Kingdom, and Japan formed a contested middle. One principal component explained 76% of variance; rankings correlated weakly with a price-versus-income measure (Spearman ρ = 0.11) and moderately with revenue-share measures (ρ = 0.48-0.64).

**Conclusions:** Multidimensional measurement reorders countries relative to price-only comparisons, separating those that pay less but reimburse quickly and invest in R&D from those that pay less, wait longer, and invest little. Code, data, and dashboard are open.

**Keywords:** pharmaceutical pricing; dynamic efficiency; composite indicator; international comparison; most-favored-nation; health technology assessment

# Highlights

- Existing measures of international "free-riding" on pharmaceutical innovation are one-dimensional, usually a price ratio, and cannot separate countries that pay less but reimburse quickly and perform R&D from those that pay less, delay access, and invest little. We built a five-component index for ten high-income countries with full uncertainty analysis.
- South Korea, Australia, and Canada rank highest and Germany and Switzerland lowest among non-US countries in almost all of 10,000 specifications; France, Italy, the United Kingdom, and Japan form a contested middle. Rankings are largely unrelated to a widely cited price-versus-income measure.
- The index, code, and dashboard give HTA agencies, payers, and negotiators a transparent, adjustable benchmark for MFN-era debates in which burden-sharing claims increasingly shape pricing and trade policy.

# Introduction

Pharmaceutical innovation is financed largely through prices paid during market exclusivity, and expected revenue is a well-documented driver of R&D effort.[1-4] Because the resulting knowledge is a global public good, countries that pay less than others benefit from research they did not fund. Basu formalized this intuition in a dynamic-efficiency model: when a large and a small market coexist, the small market can deviate from the dynamic-efficient payment rule because R&D is sustained by the larger market's payments.[5] The argument has moved from theory to policy. The May 2025 US executive order on most-favored-nation (MFN) drug pricing framed foreign prices as "freeloading",[6] the US Council of Economic Advisers expects MFN to raise prices in reference countries,[7] the United Kingdom agreed in December 2025 to increase net prices for new medicines by 25% and raise its cost-effectiveness threshold,[8] and the US Trade Representative opened a Section 301 investigation into Germany's "persistent underpayment" for innovative medicines in June 2026.[9]

Empirical measurement has not kept pace with the rhetoric. Recent quantifications each capture one dimension. Frech et al estimated each country's contribution to the global public good as price minus marginal cost times sales.[10] The US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) compared countries' shares of innovative-drug revenue with their shares of GDP and population.[11] Kolchinsky and Xie published a "Freeriding Index" based on net prices relative to income.[12] Philipson et al documented a timing channel: countries that reimburse US-first drugs years later enter after competition has eroded prices.[13] Each metric ranks countries differently, and none captures a country's contribution through domestic R&D, through fast reimbursement, or through the breadth of medicines it makes available.

Composite indicators exist precisely to summarize such multidimensional phenomena, but they are only credible when their sensitivity to normalization, weighting, and aggregation is reported.[14-17] We therefore constructed a Free-Rider Index for ten high-income countries that (1) operationalizes Basu's small-country logic with five published indicators spanning payment, access, and R&D input; (2) reports rank uncertainty across 10,000 specifications rather than a single ranking; (3) compares the result with existing single-metric measures; and (4) releases code, data, and an interactive dashboard so that decision makers can apply their own weights. Our aim is descriptive: to show which countries pay less, wait longer, or invest less relative to peers, not to adjudicate whether any level of contribution is fair.

# Methods

## Conceptual framework

In Basu's model the social planner maximizes a weighted sum of consumer and producer surplus; the canonical dynamic-efficient solution transfers the full surplus to the innovator during exclusivity, and simulations show that a small country can profitably deviate from this rule when a large country continues to pay.[5] Empirical estimates of the share of surplus that manufacturers actually capture fall well below the full-transfer benchmark.[18] Deviation can take three observable forms. A country can pay less per unit or generate less revenue relative to its economic capacity (payment domain); it can reimburse fewer new medicines or reimburse them later, reducing the present value of innovators' returns (access domain); or it can perform little pharmaceutical R&D itself, contributing less to the global knowledge stock through the "push" channel (R&D-input domain). We selected one or two indicators per domain, restricted to sources that cover all ten countries with a common definition and were published or updated in 2024-2026.

## Countries and data

The sample comprises the G7 countries plus Switzerland, Australia, and South Korea, which together account for 89% of OECD innovative-drug revenue[11] and overlap with the reference baskets used in MFN policy.[7] Table 1 lists the five components. Price level (P) is each country's price for all prescription drugs as a percentage of the US price in 2022, from RAND's bilateral, US-volume-weighted comparison of IQVIA MIDAS data (Appendix Table B.2, main specification).[19] Revenue contribution (R) is a country's share of OECD revenue from innovative branded products divided by its share of OECD GDP, aggregated over 2020-2025, from the HHS Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE).[11] Availability (A) is the share of 225 novel drugs first launched in the United States in 2014-2019 that were publicly reimbursed by 2023, and delay (D) is the mean number of years between US launch and public reimbursement, both from Philipson et al's analysis of PhRMA launch data.[13] For the United States, A and D take their reference values (100% and 0 years) by construction. R&D input (I) is business enterprise R&D performed in the pharmaceutical industry (ISIC Rev. 4 division 21) as a percentage of GDP, from the OECD BERD-by-industry database for eight countries,[20] the Office for National Statistics product-group series for the United Kingdom, whose OECD industry-classified value is not comparable,[21] and the Australian Bureau of Statistics chemical-and-pharmaceutical manufacturing line for Australia, for which no OECD value exists.[22] Because industry-association compilations of pharmaceutical R&D can differ substantially from industry-classified statistics,[23] we also assembled an association-based series (PhRMA, JPMA, EFPIA member associations, and the Canadian Patented Medicine Prices Review Board) for a sensitivity analysis. GDP and population are from the World Bank.[24] Every transcribed value, its source, and its page or table reference are listed in the repository file `manual_inputs.csv`; only publicly available aggregate data were used, so ethics review was not required.

## Index construction

Following the OECD/JRC handbook,[14] each component was oriented so that higher values indicate more apparent free-riding (P, R, A, and I were inverted), then min-max normalized to 0-100 across the ten countries. The headline index is the arithmetic mean of the five normalized components (equal weights). We also report a two-domain map that averages P and R (payment) and A and D (access) and colors countries by I.

## Robustness

We examined 12 alternative specifications: domain weights (one third per domain); payment-only, access-only, and price-only indices; geometric aggregation; z-score and rank normalization; an income-adjusted price that divides P by the ratio of a country's purchasing-power-parity GDP per capita to that of the United States; revenue contribution restricted to drugs launched after 2020; an index recomputed for the nine non-US countries, because the United States anchors the minimum of four components; and substitution of the association-based R&D series. A one-component-out jackknife tested dependence on any single indicator. Following Saisana et al,[15] we then ran 10,000 Monte Carlo specifications that jointly drew weights from a flat Dirichlet distribution, a normalization method, an aggregation rule, and independent ±10% uniform perturbations of every input, and summarized each country's rank by its median and 5th-95th percentiles. Internal coherence was assessed with Spearman correlations among components and PCA of the standardized components. Finally, we correlated the headline index with three external measures: the Kolchinsky-Xie Freeriding Index (seven overlapping countries),[12] ASPE's revenue-to-GDP and revenue-to-population ratios,[11] and Frech et al's per-capita contribution (seven countries).[10] With ten observations, correlations are descriptive and no inferential tests are reported.

## Software and transparency

Analyses used Python 3.10 with pandas, NumPy, and Matplotlib. Analysis code was drafted with the assistance of a large language model (Claude Fable 5.1, Anthropic, accessed through Claude Code in September 2026) under the author's specification; every function was reviewed, executed, and checked against hand calculations by the author, and all reported numbers are produced by the released scripts, consistent with ELEVATE-GenAI reporting guidance.[25] No AI tool generated or altered data. Code, data, tables, figures, and a self-contained interactive dashboard are available at https://github.com/YoshiSaitoDrPH/pharma-freerider-index under MIT (code) and CC BY 4.0 (documentation) licenses. Patients were not involved in this research, which uses country-level aggregates; sex and gender dimensions could not be analyzed at this level of aggregation.

# Results

## Headline index

Raw component values are shown in Table 1. Prices ranged from 34% (South Korea) to 56% (Switzerland) of US levels; the ratio of innovative-drug revenue share to GDP share ranged from 0.21 (South Korea) to 1.94 (United States); the share of US-first drugs reimbursed by 2023 ranged from 36% (Canada, South Korea) to 73% (Germany); mean reimbursement delay ranged from 1.1 years (Germany) to 3.2 years (South Korea); and pharmaceutical business R&D ranged from 0.02% (Canada) to 0.66% (Switzerland) of GDP.

Under equal weights (Figure 1A) the index ranged from 6.5 for the United States to 97.4 for South Korea. Three tiers were visible. South Korea (97.4), Australia (90.8), and Canada (89.1) scored high on every component. France (77.1), Italy (75.0), the United Kingdom (71.0), and Japan (67.8) formed a middle group whose scores were within ten points of one another. Germany (63.7) and Switzerland (56.6) scored lowest among non-US countries: Germany because it reimburses the largest share of US-first drugs with the shortest delay, Switzerland because its pharmaceutical R&D intensity is the highest in the sample. When the index was recomputed without the United States, the spread widened (Switzerland 25.7 to South Korea 97.4) but the ordering was unchanged except that the United Kingdom and Italy swapped places.

## Robustness

Across the 12 alternative specifications and five jackknife indices (Figure 2), South Korea ranked first in every case and Australia second or third in every case. Canada ranked in the top three except in the payment-only and price-only specifications, where its relatively high prices moved it to seventh. Switzerland ranked eighth or ninth in all but the access-only and R&D-excluded specifications, and Germany ranked fifth to ninth, moving up only when access components were removed or down-weighted. Spearman correlations between the headline index and alternatives were 0.94 or higher for every change in normalization, aggregation, or input definition, 0.88 when the R&D component was dropped, and 0.72-0.81 for single-domain indices (Table 2).

The Monte Carlo analysis (Figure 1B, Table 2) confirmed this structure. South Korea and Australia ranked in the top three in 99% and 100% of 10,000 specifications and Canada in 81%. Germany and Switzerland ranked in the bottom three among non-US countries in 60% and 79%. The four middle countries had 5th-95th percentile rank intervals spanning four to six positions, indicating that their relative order is a matter of weighting rather than of data. Excluding the United States did not change these intervals materially.

## Internal structure and domain map

The five components were positively correlated (Spearman ρ 0.03-0.80), with the strongest association between availability and delay (0.80) and the weakest between revenue contribution and R&D input (0.03). The first principal component explained 76% of the variance, with loadings of 0.91-0.94 on the four payment and access components and 0.61 on R&D input; the second component (15%) was dominated by R&D input (loading -0.79). Equal weights therefore approximate a one-dimensional free-riding continuum, while R&D intensity adds distinct information that mainly separates Switzerland, and to a lesser extent the United States, Germany, France, and the United Kingdom, from the rest.

The domain map (Figure 3) shows that all nine non-US countries occupy the high-payment-score half of the plane (scores 67-100), but they differ sharply on access. Germany is the only country with an access score below 50, reflecting near-universal, fast reimbursement. Canada, South Korea, and Australia combine high payment scores with the highest access scores and the lowest R&D intensities. Japan, France, the United Kingdom, Italy, and Switzerland cluster near the center of the access axis; Switzerland is distinguished only by its R&D intensity.

## Comparison with single-metric measures

The headline index was only weakly correlated with the Kolchinsky-Xie Freeriding Index across the seven overlapping countries (ρ = 0.11; Figure 4). The two measures agree that Australia ranks high but disagree most on Germany, which the price-versus-income measure ranks second-highest and our index ranks lowest among the seven, and on Canada, which it ranks lowest and our index second-highest. Correlations with ASPE's revenue-to-GDP and revenue-to-population ratios were moderate (ρ = 0.48 and 0.64 across ten countries), and with Frech et al's per-capita contribution weak (ρ = 0.36, seven countries), largely because those measures place Japan and Italy among the larger contributors whereas access and R&D indicators place them mid-table.

# Discussion

This study translates a theoretical concept, deviation from the dynamic-efficient payment rule by countries that rely on a larger market's R&D,[5] into a measurable, adjustable index. Three findings are robust to essentially any defensible analytic choice. First, South Korea, Australia, and Canada consistently combine low prices or revenue contributions with slow, partial reimbursement and low domestic pharmaceutical R&D. Second, Germany and Switzerland are the least free-riding non-US countries, but for different reasons: Germany because it grants reimbursement to almost all new medicines immediately after authorization, Switzerland because it hosts an exceptionally research-intensive industry. Third, the relative order of France, Italy, the United Kingdom, and Japan is not identifiable from these data and should not be over-interpreted.

## Why multidimensional measurement matters

The weak correlation between our index and the Kolchinsky-Xie measure is the study's most policy-relevant result. That measure, which informed the "freeloading" framing of MFN policy, is a price-versus-income calculation.[12] It ranks Germany as one of the heaviest free-riders because German list prices are low relative to German income. Our index ranks Germany lowest because it reimburses 73% of US-first drugs within about a year, contributes 4.3% of OECD innovative-drug revenue, and performs pharmaceutical R&D worth 0.15%-0.25% of GDP depending on the source. Conversely, Canada looks moderate on price alone but reimburses only 36% of US-first drugs after a mean delay of more than three years.[13] Whether delay and non-reimbursement constitute free-riding is a normative question, but from the innovator's perspective they reduce the expected return on which Basu's dynamic-efficiency condition depends, and Philipson et al show that late adopters systematically enter after US net prices have fallen.[13] A benchmark that ignores these channels rewards countries that keep list prices high while restricting access.

The comparison with revenue-share measures is also instructive. ASPE and Frech et al place Japan and Italy among the larger contributors because of their market size,[10,11] whereas our index places them mid-table because both reimburse US-first drugs after roughly two years and, in Italy's case, perform little pharmaceutical R&D. Revenue-based measures answer "how much money flows from this country to innovators?", whereas our index asks "how does this country's behavior compare with peers on each channel that affects innovators' returns?". The two questions are complementary, and the dashboard allows users to weight them as they see fit.

## Relevance to current policy

MFN implementation has made cross-country burden sharing a live negotiating variable. By September 2026, 26 manufacturers had signed MFN agreements covering most of the US branded market, the United Kingdom had agreed to raise net prices and its cost-effectiveness threshold in exchange for tariff relief,[8] and Germany faced a Section 301 investigation.[9] Analyses of the MFN benchmark itself show that Medicare savings would typically exceed the entire sales of the binding reference country, creating strong incentives for manufacturers to delay or withhold launches abroad,[26,27] a single international price would raise prices sharply outside the United States,[28] and early data suggest launches in reference countries have already fallen.[29] In this environment, a transparent index can serve three purposes. For HTA agencies and payers, it locates their own system on each channel and shows which lever (price, breadth, speed, or R&D) drives their position; the United Kingdom's decision to raise its threshold, for example, addresses the price channel but not the delay channel.[8,30] For governments negotiating with the United States, it provides a defensible alternative to price-only benchmarks. For researchers, it offers a common, reproducible baseline against which post-MFN changes can be tracked annually.

Japan illustrates the value of the multidimensional view. Its price level (51% of US) is the second-highest in the sample and its revenue contribution relative to GDP (0.73) is comparable to Italy's and Canada's, yet 56% of US-first drugs were reimbursed by 2023 and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare counts 86 medicines approved in the United States or Europe in 2016-2020 for which Japanese development had not begun.[31,32] Japan's "drug loss" is thus a problem of access rather than of price alone, consistent with its mid-table position on our index and with evidence that drugs from emerging biopharmaceutical companies are the ones most often missing.[31]

## Limitations

Several limitations warrant caution. First, the price component uses manufacturer list prices; confidential rebates are larger in the United States than elsewhere, and RAND's net-price sensitivity narrows the US gap from 278% to 173% of comparator prices.[19] Because rebates also exist in comparator countries and the direction of ranking among non-US countries is unaffected, we retained the gross series and note that the revenue component partly captures net flows. Second, the two access components derive from a single industry-compiled dataset restricted to US-first launches;[13] they may understate access in countries where drugs launch first in Europe, and the reference values assigned to the United States are definitional. The European W.A.I.T. indicator, which uses a different cohort and definition, ranks the five European countries in the same order.[33] Third, pharmaceutical R&D statistics are not harmonized: OECD industry-classified values for France and Italy are about one third of the industry-association figures, the United Kingdom and Australia required national product-based series, and clinical-trial R&D performed by contract research organizations is not captured anywhere. Substituting association-based values leaves the ranking essentially unchanged (ρ = 0.99) and dropping the component changes it modestly (ρ = 0.88), but the ±10% input noise in the Monte Carlo analysis understates this measurement uncertainty for individual countries. Fourth, the United States is the reference point for four components, so its score of 6.5 is largely definitional; the nine-country re-estimation addresses this. Fifth, the index is a cross-section drawn from sources with reference years between 2014-2019 (access cohort) and 2025 (revenue); it cannot capture the price increases that MFN agreements may induce. Sixth, cost-effectiveness thresholds, the most visible HTA lever, are not a component because only two of the ten countries state an explicit threshold and the remainder use implicit or no thresholds;[30,34] we report them descriptively in the supplement. Finally, with ten countries no inferential claims are possible, and "free-riding" is used here as a descriptive label for relative position, not as a judgment about fair contribution, which depends on ability to pay, health-system design, and the value delivered by medicines that other frameworks address.[35-37]

## Future work

The pipeline is designed for annual updating as RAND, ASPE, OECD, and reimbursement-timing data are refreshed. Natural extensions include adding R&D tax generosity and clinical-trial hosting as further contribution channels, which would help characterize countries such as Australia; extending the sample to the full MFN reference basket; and linking the index to launch-sequencing outcomes as MFN agreements mature.[38,39]

# Conclusions

A five-component Free-Rider Index built from published data identifies a stable structure among ten high-income countries: South Korea, Australia, and Canada consistently pay less, wait longer, and invest less than peers; Germany and Switzerland contribute through fast access and R&D despite low prices; and France, Italy, the United Kingdom, and Japan cannot be reliably ordered. Because the ranking diverges sharply from price-only measures, policy debates that rely on such measures risk targeting the wrong countries and the wrong levers. The open code and dashboard allow any user to test alternative weights and to track changes as MFN-era pricing evolves.
