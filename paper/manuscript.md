# Who Pays for Pharmaceutical Innovation? A Reproducible Multidimensional Free-Rider Index Across High-Income Countries

# Abstract

**Objectives:** Claims that other countries "free-ride" on pharmaceutical innovation now shape US most-favored-nation pricing policy, yet existing measures each capture one dimension and disagree. We developed a multidimensional index, tested its robustness, and drew policy implications.

**Methods:** Following Basu's dynamic-efficiency model, in which a smaller market can free-ride on research and development (R&D) induced by a larger market, we combined five published indicators for nine high-income countries (G7 excluding the United States, Switzerland, Australia, South Korea), with the United States as reference: brand-name drug price level, innovative-drug revenue share relative to gross domestic product (GDP) share, share of US-first new drugs publicly reimbursed and mean interval to reimbursement, and pharmaceutical R&D as a percentage of GDP. Components were min-max normalized and equally weighted; robustness was tested with 21 alternative specifications, 10,000 Monte Carlo draws with calibrated noise and alternative sources, principal component analysis, and comparison with single-metric measures.

**Results:** Scores ranged from 27 (Switzerland) to 97 (South Korea). South Korea and Australia ranked in the top three in 99% and 84% of draws and Germany in the bottom three in 85%; Canada, France, Japan, the United Kingdom, and Italy formed a contested middle. The payment components tracked a price-based "Freeriding Index" (Spearman ρ = 0.89), whereas the adoption components showed no rank correlation with it (ρ = -0.04).

**Conclusions:** Payment, adoption, and R&D profiles diverge, and price-only comparisons conflate them. The index describes relative position, not deviation from an efficient contribution; reconciling individually rational under-contribution with collectively efficient innovation is the policy question.

**Keywords:** pharmaceutical pricing; dynamic efficiency; composite indicator; international comparison; most-favored-nation; health technology assessment

# Highlights

- Existing measures of international "free-riding" on pharmaceutical innovation each capture one dimension (price, revenue, or timing) and disagree. We built a five-component index (payment, adoption, R&D) for nine high-income countries with calibrated uncertainty analysis and open code.
- South Korea and Australia rank highest and Germany and Switzerland lowest in most of 10,000 specifications; the middle is contested. A price-based measure tracks the payment components but not the adoption components, on which countries differ most.
- The index measures relative position, not deviation from an efficient contribution. Because R&D responds to expected revenue in every market while funding is concentrated in one national market, the results frame a collective-action problem for payers and HTA bodies outside the United States.

# Introduction

Pharmaceutical innovation is financed largely through prices paid during market exclusivity, and expected revenue is a well-documented driver of R&D.[1-5] Because the resulting knowledge is a global public good, countries that pay less than others benefit from research they did not fund. Basu formalized this intuition in a dynamic-efficiency model: when a large and a small market coexist, the small market can deviate from the dynamic-efficient payment rule because R&D is sustained by the larger market's payments.[6] Public-goods theory predicts this equilibrium: smaller contributors under-provide and the largest is "exploited".[7,8] The May 2025 US executive order on most-favored-nation (MFN) drug pricing framed foreign prices as "freeloading"[9] and is expected to raise prices in reference countries,[10] the United Kingdom agreed in December 2025 to raise its cost-effectiveness threshold, which with a new value set the government equates to about 25% higher prices for new medicines,[11] and the US Trade Representative opened a Section 301 investigation into Germany's "persistent underpayment" for innovative medicines in June 2026.[12]

Measurement has not kept pace; recent quantifications each capture one dimension. Frech et al estimated each country's contribution to the global public good as price minus marginal cost times sales and found that country size largely determines it.[8] The US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) compared countries' shares of innovative-drug revenue with their shares of GDP and population.[13] Kolchinsky and Xie published a "Freeriding Index" based on net brand prices relative to income.[14] Philipson et al documented a timing channel: countries that reimburse US-first drugs years later enter after competition has eroded prices.[15] Each ranks countries differently, and none captures contribution through domestic R&D or through fast, broad reimbursement.

Composite indicators summarize such phenomena but are credible only when their sensitivity to normalization, weighting, and aggregation is reported.[16-19] We therefore constructed a Free-Rider Index that (1) operationalizes the small-country logic with five published indicators spanning payment, adoption, and R&D input; (2) reports rank uncertainty across thousands of specifications rather than a single ranking; (3) decomposes its relationship with existing single-metric measures; and (4) releases code, data, and an interactive dashboard so that decision makers can apply their own weights. The term "free-riding" is adopted from the literature under review.[6,8,14] Three levels of claim should be kept apart: that countries differ on measurable payment, adoption, and R&D characteristics (shown directly); that these bear on global innovation incentives (theory and prior evidence); and that a particular country contributes too little (not established here).

# Methods

## Conceptual framework

In Basu's model a social payer maximizes a weighted sum of consumer and producer surplus (weight λ on consumers), and innovators respond to the share β of surplus they appropriate during exclusivity. The optimal share is β* = λ/[(2λ−1)(1+ηε)], where η and ε describe the innovation production function; full transfer (β* = 1) is optimal whenever λ ≤ 0.5, and with the estimated technology β* falls to 0.26 when only consumer surplus counts.[6] In a two-country simulation, requiring the small country to pay the full share raises global output from 46 to 49 new drugs a year and benefits the large country, but lowers the small country's own long-run consumer surplus by 20%.[6] Empirical estimates of the share manufacturers capture fall far below full transfer: about 5% for HIV drugs,[20] a median of about 15% across technologies,[21] and medians of 13%-15% in a 2026 systematic review,[22] while a health-maximizing planner would offer roughly 20% of the value of new medicines (22% excluding effects on innovation quality, 11% including them).[23] β is not observed for any country; our indicators are observable correlates of the channels through which β operates, and the index is a relative scale, not a distance from an efficient benchmark. Two domains map onto the expected-revenue mechanism: a country can pay less for on-patent medicines or generate less innovative-drug revenue relative to its economic capacity (payment), or reimburse fewer new medicines later, reducing the present value of innovators' returns (adoption). A third domain, domestic pharmaceutical R&D, is our extension: in the model, market rewards cause industry R&D, so domestic R&D is neither part of β nor a payer's transfer. We include it because contribution to the global stock of innovation runs through research capacity as well as payment, and omitting it would score research-intensive but low-priced countries purely as free-riders; the cost is that the composite mixes payment with activity, so we also report the four-component payment-and-adoption index and read R&D as where research is performed, not who funds it. Two caveats follow. Lower prices in lower-income or lower-threshold markets can be efficient under Ramsey or value-based differential pricing,[24,25] and paying up to the health-opportunity-cost threshold is not under-payment relative to domestic value,[26] so the payment components measure relative payment. Launch and reimbursement timing reflect manufacturers' sequencing and regulatory review as well as payer behavior,[27-29] so the adoption components measure outcomes. Equal component weights give the domains 40%, 40%, and 20%; equal domain weights are the first sensitivity analysis. Indicators come from sources updated in 2024-2026 covering all ten countries with a common definition.

## Countries and data

The sample is the G7 plus Switzerland, Australia, and South Korea, which account for 91% of OECD revenue from innovative branded drugs in 2020-2025[13] and overlap with the MFN reference baskets.[10,30] Because the United States defines the reference values for price, availability, and interval, it is the numeraire and is not ranked; a ten-country variant is a sensitivity analysis. Table 1 lists the five components. Price level (P) is each country's brand-name originator price as a percentage of the US price in 2022, from RAND's bilateral, US-volume-weighted comparison of IQVIA MIDAS manufacturer prices;[31] the all-drug index, which mixes in generics, is a sensitivity analysis. Revenue contribution (R) is a country's share of OECD revenue from innovative branded products divided by its share of OECD GDP, aggregated over 2020-2025, from the HHS Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE).[13] Availability (A) is the share of 225 novel drugs first launched in the United States in 2014-2019 that were publicly reimbursed by 2023, and interval (D) the mean years from US launch to public reimbursement among drugs reimbursed after the US launch, both from Philipson et al's analysis of the industry-compiled PhRMA launch dataset (no microdata released);[15] D is conditional on reimbursement and spans filing, review, and payer decision (Appendix Table S8). R&D input (I) is pharmaceutical business enterprise R&D (ISIC Rev. 4 division 21) as a percentage of GDP, from the OECD for eight countries,[32] the Office for National Statistics product-group series for the United Kingdom, whose OECD value is not comparable,[33] and the Australian Bureau of Statistics line for basic chemical and chemical product manufacturing (ANZSIC 18, an upper bound).[34] Government health-R&D budgets are not comparable where universities are block-funded (Appendix Table S1); an industry-association series[35] and industry-sponsored trial starts per million population (ClinicalTrials.gov) are alternatives. GDP and population are from the World Bank.[36] Every transcribed value and its source are listed in the repository; only public aggregate data were used.

## Index construction

Following the OECD/JRC handbook,[16] each component was oriented so that higher values indicate more apparent free-riding (P, R, A, and I inverted) and min-max normalized to 0-100 across the nine countries; the headline index is their equal-weight arithmetic mean. Because nominal weights do not equal importance,[19] we report each component's squared Pearson correlation with the composite, the linear case of the correlation-ratio importance measure of Becker et al[19] and Paruolo et al,[37] labelled effective importance.

## Robustness

We examined 21 alternative specifications (Table 2): domain weights; single-domain and four-component indices; geometric aggregation; z-score, rank, and log min-max normalization; the all-drug price index; income-adjusted prices and an ability-to-pay-adjusted revenue ratio assuming, as a stylized progressive benchmark in the spirit of income-related differential pricing,[24,25] an income elasticity of 1.3; post-2020 revenue; an interval assigning non-reimbursed drugs the 6.5-year follow-up horizon; EFPIA W.A.I.T. adoption data for the five European countries;[38] alternative R&D indicators (association series, trial hosting, tax subsidy,[39] omission); residualization on log GDP;[7,8] and inclusion of the United States. We also dropped each component and each country in turn. Following Saisana et al,[17] 10,000 Monte Carlo specifications jointly drew weights from a flat Dirichlet distribution, a normalization method, an aggregation rule, calibrated input noise (Table 2), and, for P, R, and I, a data source; we report median ranks, 5th-95th percentiles, and shares of specifications in the top and bottom three (frequencies under this prior, not probabilities), and repeat the exercise varying one source of uncertainty at a time and with correlated noise (ρ = ±0.7) for the two adoption components, which share a source. A uniform gross-to-net adjustment of US prices cannot change the nine-country ranking, because min-max normalization is invariant to common scaling. Internal coherence was assessed with Spearman correlations among components and with log GDP, and with principal component analysis (PCA). Finally, we correlated the headline index and its payment and adoption sub-indices with the Kolchinsky-Xie Freeriding Index (seven overlapping countries),[14] ASPE's revenue-to-GDP and revenue-to-population ratios,[13] and Frech et al's per-capita contribution,[8] with Fisher intervals and permutation p values.

## Software and transparency

Analyses used Python 3.10. Code was drafted with a large language model (Claude Fable 5.1, Anthropic, via Claude Code, September-October 2026) under the first author's specification; the authors reviewed every function and checked it against hand calculations, a second author independently verified every transcribed value against its source, and all reported numbers are produced by the released scripts (ELEVATE-GenAI guidance).[40] No AI tool generated or altered data. Code, data, tables, figures, and a self-contained interactive dashboard are openly available [repository address withheld for anonymized review; MIT license for code, CC BY 4.0 for documentation]. Patients were not involved; sex and gender cannot be analyzed in country-level aggregates.

# Results

## Headline index

Brand-name prices ranged from 14% (South Korea) to 31% (Canada) of US levels (Table 1); the revenue-to-GDP-share ratio from 0.21 (South Korea) to 0.76 (Italy), against 1.94 for the United States; the share of US-first drugs reimbursed by 2023 from 36% (Canada, South Korea) to 73% (Germany); the mean interval from 1.1 years (Germany) to 3.2 years (South Korea); and pharmaceutical R&D from 0.02% (Canada) to 0.66% (Switzerland) of GDP.

Under equal weights (Figure 1A) the index ranged from 27.4 for Switzerland to 97.4 for South Korea. South Korea scored highest on every component except R&D, on which it ranked fifth. Australia (74.8) combined the second-lowest brand price and revenue ratio with slow, partial reimbursement and low R&D. Canada (59.9) had the highest brand price and second-highest revenue ratio among the nine but reimbursed the fewest US-first drugs, after the second-longest interval, with the lowest R&D intensity. France (48.7), Japan (42.7), the United Kingdom (40.2), and Italy (35.8) followed. Germany (28.5) and Switzerland (27.4) scored lowest, Germany through the broadest and fastest reimbursement and Switzerland through the highest R&D intensity. Scoring the United States as a tenth unit compressed the nine into 59.7-97.4 (United States 6.5) and swapped only Japan and the United Kingdom.

## Robustness

Across the 21 alternative specifications and five component-omission indices (Figure 2), South Korea ranked first and Australia second or third in every case. Canada ranked third in most specifications but ninth on payment components alone. Germany ranked eighth or ninth except in payment-only and price-only indices (fifth) and rank normalization (seventh); Switzerland except on adoption components alone (fourth) or income-adjusted prices (fifth), where its high income makes its prices look low. The four-component payment-and-adoption index preserved the order except that Switzerland moved from ninth to seventh and Germany to ninth (ρ = 0.95). Spearman correlations with the headline were at least 0.95 for every change in normalization, data source, or sample except geometric aggregation (0.87), income-adjusted payment (0.82), post-2020 revenue (0.92), and added trial hosting or tax subsidies (0.87-0.90); single-domain indices gave 0.53-0.72 (Table 2). Substituting trial hosting for R&D input moved Japan, which hosts the fewest industry trials per capita (3.4 starts per million per year, against 21.5 in Australia), from fifth to third. Dropping each country in turn left the top and bottom two unchanged and reordered the middle (Appendix Table S10).

The Monte Carlo analysis (Figure 1B, Table 2) showed a stable top and bottom around a contested middle. South Korea and Australia ranked in the top three in 99% and 84% of specifications; Germany ranked in the bottom three in 85% and Switzerland in 67%. Canada was in the top three in 54% and Italy and Japan in the bottom three in 49% and 36%, with 5th-95th percentile rank intervals spanning six to eight positions. Varying one source of uncertainty at a time (Appendix Table S9b), the middle ranks moved mainly with weights and data source, whereas measurement noise, including correlated noise, and the normalization and aggregation rules left the top and bottom three nearly fixed.

## Internal structure

The five components were only moderately correlated (Spearman ρ from -0.25 to 0.73): price with revenue contribution (0.63) and availability with interval (0.73), but the payment and adoption domains weakly with each other (0.17-0.35), and R&D input was uncorrelated with price (0.02) and negatively correlated with revenue contribution (-0.25). The first principal component explained 53% of the variance and the second, which separated payment from adoption, 22%; with the United States included, the first component explained 76%, a one-dimensionality produced by the outlier. Effective importance was 0.65-0.66 for adoption, 0.51-0.55 for payment, and 0.24 for R&D input. Availability and interval were negatively associated with country size (ρ = -0.63 and -0.50 with log GDP), brand price was not (0.23), and residualizing on log GDP left the ranking unchanged.

## Comparison with single-metric measures

The headline index was weakly and imprecisely correlated with the Kolchinsky-Xie Freeriding Index across seven overlapping countries (ρ = 0.25; Fisher 95% interval -0.62 to 0.84). Decomposition explains why (Figure 3): the payment sub-index tracked the price-based measure closely (ρ = 0.89; interval 0.43 to 0.98), as expected from shared inputs, whereas the adoption sub-index showed no rank correlation (ρ = -0.04; interval -0.77 to 0.74). The measures agree on Australia but disagree on Germany (near the top on the price-based measure, lowest on ours) and Canada (lowest there, third on ours). Correlations with ASPE's revenue-to-GDP and revenue-to-population ratios and with Frech et al's per-capita contribution were 0.35, 0.60, and 0.57 (nine countries; Appendix Table S6), all imprecise; those revenue-based measures place Japan and Italy among the larger contributors whereas adoption and R&D place them mid-table.

# Discussion

This study translates the channels through which, in Basu's model, a country can rely on a larger market's R&D[6] into a measurable, adjustable index of relative position. Three findings hold across specifications. First, South Korea and Australia combine low brand prices and revenue contributions with slow, partial reimbursement, whereas Germany and Switzerland score lowest through the broadest and fastest reimbursement and through research intensity, respectively. Second, the middle is contested: Canada pays the most among non-US countries for brand-name medicines but reimburses the fewest US-first drugs after the second-longest interval; Japan pays mid-range prices but hosts the fewest trials; France, the United Kingdom, and Italy cannot be ordered. Third, the payment and adoption channels were only weakly correlated in this sample, so price-based and adoption-based measures rank the same countries differently.

## Why multidimensional measurement matters

The decomposition in Figure 3 is the most policy-relevant result. The Kolchinsky-Xie measure, which shares the "freeloading" framing of MFN policy, is a net brand-price calculation adjusted for income;[14] our payment sub-index tracks it closely, whereas the adoption sub-index carries information it does not contain. Germany ranks near the top of the price-based measure because its prices are low relative to income, but lowest among the seven countries common to both measures (Switzerland, lowest among the nine, is absent from the price-based measure) because it reimburses 73% of US-first drugs, the largest share, after 1.1 years and contributes 4.3% of OECD innovative-drug revenue. Canada is the mirror image: highest brand price, lowest reimbursement of US-first drugs (36%) after more than three years.[15] For innovators, non-reimbursement and delay reduce expected returns, and late adopters enter after US net prices have fallen.[15] Whether delay is the payer's doing is a separate question: manufacturers file late or not at all in low-price and heavily referenced markets,[27-29,41,42] submission delays vary across countries more than review times,[43] and in Japan listing follows approval within 60 to 90 days,[44] so the interval there arises mainly before the payer decision. The adoption components describe the joint outcome of payer, regulator, and firm. Revenue-share measures are complementary: they ask how much money flows from a country, largely a function of size,[8] whereas our index compares countries on each channel.

## Dynamic efficiency as a shared responsibility

Basu's two-country simulation is explicit: when the small country adopts the full-transfer rule, global output rises by about three new drugs a year and the large country gains, but the small country's long-run consumer surplus falls by a fifth.[6] In the Olson-Zeckhauser equilibrium that Frech et al apply to pharmaceuticals, the public good is under-supplied and the largest contributor exploited.[7,8] The index cannot show that any country pays too little, nor does Basu's model show that a small country gains from paying more. The case for raising contributions outside the United States rests on three considerations beyond it.

First, the model holds the large country's rule fixed, an assumption now in doubt: under MFN, Medicare savings would exceed manufacturers' entire annual sales in the binding reference country for 73% (101 of 138) of drugs with international sales data,[45] a single international price at constant profits would raise prices outside the United States by 28% to more than 300%,[46] and European reference pricing produced convergence and delayed access.[47] If US willingness to pay falls, the innovation the small country relied on goes unfinanced. Second, the model assumes approved drugs reach every market; in practice lower expected prices delay or foreclose launches,[27-29,41,42] Basu himself hypothesizes that manufacturers will delay or forgo launches in referenced markets rather than accept discounts that erode US revenue,[6] early data show launches falling in referenced markets,[30] MFN benchmarks rest on small markets,[48] and Japan's drug loss (52% of US-approved drugs from emerging biopharmaceutical companies, against 19% in Europe[49]) shows the mechanism at work. Third, R&D responds to expected revenue everywhere: a 1% increase in potential market size is associated with 4%-6% more new molecular entities,[1] the elasticity of new chemical entities to expected global revenue is about 0.23, or roughly US$2.5 billion per additional entity,[2] Medicare Part D raised pipeline entry,[3,50] low-reimbursement US Medicaid showed no response,[51] and China's reimbursement expansion was followed by an 86% relative rise in domestic trials.[52] A review places long-run elasticities to US revenue at 0.25-1.5.[53] As an order of magnitude, raising each of the nine countries' revenue-to-GDP ratio to the current OECD average would add about 16% to OECD innovative-drug revenue (26% if parity is defined on post-change shares), implying roughly 4%-39% more new drugs per year at those elasticities; this is a long-run scenario under strong assumptions (GDP proportionality as benchmark; unchanged volumes, prices elsewhere, and value of what is developed; more drugs, not more health) detailed in Appendix Table S11.

Contribution need not mean paying US prices: Germany and Switzerland reach low scores through fast listing and domestic R&D, and trial hosting is a further channel. More revenue raises R&D, but more R&D is not more health. That the optimal share of value to offer manufacturers can lie below current payments,[23] that demand pull disproportionately funds incremental products,[50,54] that empirical opportunity-cost thresholds lie below those payers apply,[26,55] that R&D outlays do not explain prices,[56] and that pharmaceutical margins exceed other large companies' even net of R&D[57] together argue for value-based contributions paired with transparent HTA and push instruments, not unconditional price increases. The problem the index frames is one of collective action: individually rational strategies leave global innovation under-financed and exposed to a single national market, and theory points to coordination, through negotiated contributions, joint procurement, pooled pull commitments, or push funding, rather than unilateral referencing alone.[8,46,58]

## Relevance to current policy

By September 2026, 26 manufacturers had signed MFN agreements covering 89% of the US branded market,[59] the United Kingdom had agreed to a NICE threshold of £25,000-35,000 per quality-adjusted life-year, equated by the government to about 25% higher prices for new medicines, in a deal that included a 0% US tariff on its pharmaceutical exports,[11,60] and Germany faced a Section 301 investigation.[12] A transparent index locates each system on each channel (the UK change raises the threshold NICE applies,[60] a value benchmark for price[61]; its effect on reimbursement timing remains to be observed), offers an alternative to price-only benchmarks with explicit weights, and gives a baseline for tracking post-MFN change.

Japan illustrates the point: mid-range brand price (22% of US) and revenue ratio (0.73), yet only 56% of US-first drugs reimbursed by 2023 and the fewest industry trials per capita. Of 143 medicines approved in the United States or Europe in 2016-2020 that remained unapproved in Japan at the end of 2022, 86 had no Japanese development under way as of March 2023,[44,62,63] for which the ministry's 2023 expert panel report cited price cuts, low pricing predictability, low growth expectations, and regulatory data requirements.[44] Drug loss enters the index through adoption, not payment.

## Limitations

Several limitations warrant caution. First, the index is relative: some country is always scored as the heaviest free-rider even if all contribute adequately, and it cannot say how positions would change if US willingness to pay fell or how far one country's under-contribution is offset by others; it operationalizes the channels in Basu's model but does not test it. Second, prices are gross manufacturer prices before rebates. A uniform US gross-to-net adjustment, such as RAND's 37.7% reduction,[31] leaves the nine-country ranking unchanged by construction; differing confidential rebates among comparator systems would move it but could not be quantified. Third, the adoption components derive from one industry-compiled dataset of US-first launches;[15] they may understate adoption where drugs launch first in Europe, count products rather than clinical value, so declining low-benefit products scores as free-riding,[64-66] exclude early-access schemes, and reflect firms' launch strategies and regulatory timing as well as payer decisions. The European W.A.I.T. indicator is broadly concordant (rank correlation 0.70; Appendix Table S7) and substituting it left the ranking unchanged. Fourth, the interval is conditional on reimbursement and follow-up differs by launch year; imputing the horizon to non-reimbursed drugs changed no rank (S13), but only drug-level data would resolve the censoring. Fifth, pharmaceutical R&D statistics are not harmonized: OECD values for France and Italy are about one third of association figures, the United Kingdom and Australia required national series, and contract-research R&D is not captured; the component determines Switzerland's position, and because differences between series are definitional, the data-source draws in the Monte Carlo analysis are scenario comparisons rather than sampling uncertainty. Sixth, the France-Japan-United Kingdom-Italy block reorders when countries are removed. Seventh, the index is a cross-section whose components refer to different years, from 2014-2019 (adoption) to 2025 (revenue); it cannot capture MFN-induced price increases, and future adoption scores will partly reflect MFN-driven launch delays.[29,30,42,48] Eighth, cost-effectiveness thresholds are described, not scored (Appendix Table S5). Finally, nine countries permit no inferential claims; disease-area heterogeneity, affordability, and public research funding are not captured; and "free-riding" is a descriptive label for relative position, not a judgment about fair contribution, which depends on ability to pay, system design, and the value medicines deliver.[24-26,55]

## Future work

Priorities are fixed reference bounds so that annual updates track change within countries; decomposing the interval into sponsor filing, regulatory review, and payer decision; a present-value measure of expected revenue (price, volume, adoption timing, remaining exclusivity) closer to Basu's β; value-weighted availability; and extension to the full MFN reference basket.

# Conclusions

A five-component Free-Rider Index built from published data gives a stable top and bottom among nine high-income countries: South Korea and Australia combine low payment with slow, partial adoption; Germany and Switzerland contribute through fast listing and research intensity; the other five occupy a middle whose order depends on the weighted channel. Because the payment and adoption channels were only weakly related in this sample, price-only measures cannot show which channel differs or which lever matters. The index describes relative position, not deviation from an efficient contribution. Basu's model shows why under-contribution is individually rational for smaller markets; the concentration of funding in one national market, the launch consequences of low expected returns, and the responsiveness of R&D to revenue everywhere show why it is collectively costly. Reconciling the two is a coordination problem, and the index is a transparent starting point.
