# Who Pays for Pharmaceutical Innovation? A Reproducible Multidimensional Free-Rider Index for Ten High-Income Countries

# Abstract

**Objectives:** Claims that other countries "free-ride" on pharmaceutical innovation now shape US most-favored-nation pricing policy, yet existing measures rank countries on one dimension, usually price. We developed a multidimensional index, tested its robustness, and drew implications for shared investment in innovation.

**Methods:** Following Basu's dynamic-efficiency model, in which a smaller market can free-ride on research and development (R&D) induced by a larger market, we combined five published indicators for nine high-income countries (G7 excluding the United States, Switzerland, Australia, South Korea), with the United States as reference: brand-name drug price level (2022), innovative-drug revenue share relative to gross domestic product (GDP) share (2020-2025), share of US-first new drugs publicly reimbursed and mean interval to reimbursement, and pharmaceutical R&D as a percentage of GDP. Components were min-max normalized and equally weighted; robustness was assessed with 22 alternative specifications, 10,000 Monte Carlo specifications with calibrated noise and alternative sources, principal component analysis, and comparison with single-metric measures.

**Results:** Scores (0-100) ranged from 27 (Switzerland) to 97 (South Korea). South Korea and Australia ranked in the top three in 99% and 83% of specifications and Germany in the bottom three in 88%; Canada, France, Japan, the United Kingdom, and Italy occupied a contested middle. The payment components reproduced a price-based "Freeriding Index" (Spearman ρ = 0.89), whereas the adoption components were unrelated to it (ρ = -0.04).

**Conclusions:** Multidimensional measurement separates payment, adoption, and R&D channels that price-only comparisons conflate. Because R&D responds to expected revenue in every market and funding is concentrated in one payer, dynamic efficiency requires each country to raise its contribution.

**Keywords:** pharmaceutical pricing; dynamic efficiency; composite indicator; international comparison; most-favored-nation; health technology assessment

# Highlights

- Existing measures of international "free-riding" on pharmaceutical innovation are one-dimensional, usually a price ratio. We built a five-component index (payment, adoption, R&D) for nine high-income countries with calibrated uncertainty analysis and open code.
- South Korea and Australia rank highest and Germany lowest in most of 10,000 specifications; the middle is contested. Price-based measures capture the payment channel but not adoption or R&D, on which countries differ most.
- Because R&D responds to expected revenue in every market and funding is concentrated in the United States, payers and HTA bodies elsewhere have a direct stake in raising their contribution through payment for value, faster listing, and domestic R&D.

# Introduction

Pharmaceutical innovation is financed largely through prices paid during market exclusivity, and expected revenue is a well-documented driver of R&D effort.[1-5] Because the resulting knowledge is a global public good, countries that pay less than others benefit from research they did not fund. Basu formalized this intuition in a dynamic-efficiency model: when a large and a small market coexist, the small market can deviate from the dynamic-efficient payment rule because R&D is sustained by the larger market's payments.[6] Public-goods theory predicts the same outcome as an equilibrium, not an aberration: smaller contributors under-provide and the largest contributor is "exploited".[7,8] The argument has moved from theory to policy. The May 2025 US executive order on most-favored-nation (MFN) drug pricing framed foreign prices as "freeloading",[9] the Council of Economic Advisers expects MFN to raise prices in reference countries,[10] the United Kingdom agreed in December 2025 to raise net prices for new medicines and its cost-effectiveness threshold,[11,12] and the US Trade Representative opened a Section 301 investigation into Germany's "persistent underpayment" for innovative medicines in June 2026.[13]

Empirical measurement has not kept pace with the rhetoric. Recent quantifications each capture one dimension. Frech et al estimated each country's contribution to the global public good as price minus marginal cost times sales and found that country size largely determines it.[8] The US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) compared countries' shares of innovative-drug revenue with their shares of GDP and population.[14] Kolchinsky and Xie published a "Freeriding Index" based on net brand prices relative to income.[15] Philipson et al documented a timing channel: countries that reimburse US-first drugs years later enter after competition has eroded prices.[16] Each metric ranks countries differently, and none captures contribution through domestic R&D, fast reimbursement, or the breadth of medicines made available.

Composite indicators exist to summarize such multidimensional phenomena, but they are credible only when their sensitivity to normalization, weighting, and aggregation is reported.[17-20] We therefore constructed a Free-Rider Index that (1) operationalizes the small-country logic with five published indicators spanning payment, adoption, and R&D input; (2) reports rank uncertainty across thousands of specifications rather than a single ranking; (3) decomposes its relationship with existing single-metric measures; and (4) releases code, data, and an interactive dashboard so that decision makers can apply their own weights. The term "free-riding" is adopted from the literature under review;[6,8,15] the measurement is descriptive, and we return to its normative interpretation in the Discussion.

# Methods

## Conceptual framework

In Basu's model the social planner maximizes a weighted sum of consumer and producer surplus; the canonical dynamic-efficient solution transfers the full surplus to the innovator during exclusivity, and simulations show that a small country can profitably deviate from this rule when a large country continues to pay.[6] Empirical estimates of the share of surplus that manufacturers actually capture cluster far below this benchmark, at roughly 5%-15%.[21-23] Deviation can take three observable forms. A country can pay less for on-patent medicines or generate less innovative-drug revenue relative to its economic capacity (payment domain); it can reimburse fewer new medicines or reimburse them later, reducing the present value of innovators' returns (adoption domain); or it can perform little pharmaceutical R&D itself, contributing less through the "push" channel (R&D-input domain). Three caveats frame the interpretation. Lower prices in lower-income or lower-threshold markets can be efficient under Ramsey or value-based differential pricing,[24,25] and a system paying up to its empirical health-opportunity-cost threshold is not under-paying relative to domestic value;[26] the payment components therefore measure relative payment, not under-payment relative to value. Launch and reimbursement timing reflect manufacturers' sequencing and regulatory review as well as payer behavior,[27-29] so the adoption components measure outcomes, not intent. Business R&D located in a country reflects firms' location decisions financed by global revenue, so the R&D component measures where research is performed, not who pays. We selected one or two indicators per domain from sources published or updated in 2024-2026 that cover all ten countries with a common definition.

## Countries and data

The sample comprises the G7 countries plus Switzerland, Australia, and South Korea, which account for 91% of OECD innovative-drug revenue[14] and overlap with MFN reference baskets.[10] Because the United States defines the reference values for price, availability, and interval, it serves as the numeraire and is not ranked; the headline index is computed across the nine other countries, and a ten-country variant is reported as a sensitivity analysis. Table 1 lists the five components. Price level (P) is each country's price for brand-name originator drugs as a percentage of the US price in 2022, from RAND's bilateral, US-volume-weighted comparison of IQVIA MIDAS manufacturer prices (Appendix Table B.1);[30] the all-drug index, which mixes generics on which the United States is cheaper, is used only as a sensitivity analysis. Revenue contribution (R) is a country's share of OECD revenue from innovative branded products divided by its share of OECD GDP, aggregated over 2020-2025, from the HHS Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE).[14] Availability (A) is the share of 225 novel drugs first launched in the United States in 2014-2019 that were publicly reimbursed by 2023, and interval (D) is the mean number of years from US launch to public reimbursement among reimbursed drugs, both from Philipson et al's analysis of the industry-compiled PhRMA launch dataset, for which no microdata are released;[16] D is conditional on reimbursement and the interval includes sponsor filing, regulatory review, and payer decision (Appendix Table S8 gives each country's operational definition). R&D input (I) is business enterprise R&D performed in the pharmaceutical industry (ISIC Rev. 4 division 21) as a percentage of GDP, from the OECD BERD-by-industry database for eight countries,[31] the Office for National Statistics product-group series for the United Kingdom, whose OECD industry-classified value is not comparable,[32] and the Australian Bureau of Statistics line for basic chemical and chemical product manufacturing (ANZSIC subdivision 18, an upper bound) for Australia, for which no OECD value exists.[33] Government health-R&D budgets were examined as a public "push" alternative but are not comparable where universities are block-funded (Appendix Table S1); an industry-association R&D series[34] and industry-sponsored trial starts per million population (ClinicalTrials.gov) serve as alternatives. GDP and population are from the World Bank.[35] Every transcribed value, its source, and its page or table reference are listed in the repository; only publicly available aggregate data were used, so ethics review was not required.

## Index construction

Following the OECD/JRC handbook,[17] each component was oriented so that higher values indicate more apparent free-riding (P, R, A, and I were inverted), then min-max normalized to 0-100 across the nine countries. The headline index is the arithmetic mean of the five normalized components (equal weights). Because nominal weights do not equal importance,[20] we report each component's effective importance, the squared correlation between the normalized component and the composite.[36]

## Robustness

We examined 22 alternative specifications, defined in Table 2: domain weights; single-domain indices; geometric aggregation; z-score, rank, and log min-max normalization; the all-drug price index; income- and ability-to-pay-adjusted payment components with a progressive income elasticity of 1.3;[24,25] a US net-price adjustment applying RAND's 37.7% gross-to-net reduction;[30] revenue restricted to post-2020 launches; an interval that assigns non-reimbursed drugs the 6.5-year follow-up horizon; the EFPIA Patients W.A.I.T. adoption data for the five European countries;[37] alternative R&D indicators (association series, trial hosting, tax subsidy,[38] omission); residualization on log GDP to separate contribution from size;[7,8] and inclusion of the United States as a tenth scored unit. We also dropped each component in turn and each country in turn. Following Saisana et al,[18] we then ran 10,000 Monte Carlo specifications that jointly drew weights from a flat Dirichlet distribution, a normalization method, an aggregation rule, calibrated input noise (Table 2), and, for P, R, and I, a randomly chosen data source; we report each country's median rank, 5th-95th percentiles, and the share of specifications in the top and bottom three, which are frequencies under this prior rather than probabilities. Internal coherence was assessed with Spearman correlations among components, correlations with log GDP, and principal component analysis (PCA). Finally, we correlated the headline index and its payment and adoption sub-indices with the Kolchinsky-Xie Freeriding Index (seven overlapping countries),[15] ASPE's revenue-to-GDP and revenue-to-population ratios,[14] and Frech et al's per-capita contribution (six countries),[8] reporting Fisher intervals and permutation p values because, with nine or fewer observations, such correlations are imprecise.

## Software and transparency

Analyses used Python 3.10 with pandas, NumPy, and Matplotlib. Analysis code was drafted with the assistance of a large language model (Claude Fable 5.1, Anthropic, accessed through Claude Code in September 2026) under the author's specification; every function was reviewed, executed, and checked against hand calculations by the author, all data values were transcribed from or verified against the cited source documents by the author, and all reported numbers are produced by the released scripts, consistent with ELEVATE-GenAI reporting guidance.[39] No AI tool generated or altered data. Code, data, tables, figures, and a self-contained interactive dashboard are openly available [repository address withheld for anonymized review; MIT license for code, CC BY 4.0 for documentation]. Patients were not involved in this research, which uses country-level aggregates; sex and gender dimensions could not be analyzed at this level of aggregation.

# Results

## Headline index

Raw component values are shown in Table 1. Brand-name prices ranged from 14% (South Korea) to 31% (Canada) of US levels; the ratio of innovative-drug revenue share to GDP share ranged from 0.21 (South Korea) to 0.76 (Italy), against 1.94 for the United States; the share of US-first drugs reimbursed by 2023 ranged from 36% (Canada, South Korea) to 73% (Germany); the mean interval to reimbursement ranged from 1.1 years (Germany) to 3.2 years (South Korea); and pharmaceutical business R&D ranged from 0.02% (Canada) to 0.66% (Switzerland) of GDP.

Under equal weights (Figure 1A) the index ranged from 27.4 for Switzerland to 97.4 for South Korea. South Korea scored highest on every component except R&D, on which it ranked fifth. Australia (74.8) combined the second-lowest brand price and revenue ratio with slow, partial reimbursement and low R&D. Canada (59.9) had the highest brand price and second-highest revenue ratio among the nine countries but reimbursed the fewest US-first drugs after the longest interval and had the lowest R&D intensity. France (48.7), Japan (42.7), the United Kingdom (40.2), and Italy (35.8) followed. Germany (28.5) and Switzerland (27.4) scored lowest: Germany because it reimburses the largest share of US-first drugs after the shortest interval, Switzerland because its R&D intensity is the highest in the sample. Scoring the United States as a tenth unit compressed the nine countries into the upper part of the scale (59.7-97.4; United States 6.5) without changing their order except for Japan and the United Kingdom.

## Robustness

Across the 22 alternative specifications and five component-omission indices (Figure 2), South Korea ranked first in every case and Australia second or third in every case. Canada ranked third in most specifications but ninth when only payment components were used. Germany ranked eighth or ninth except in payment-only and price-only indices (fifth) and rank normalization (seventh). Switzerland ranked eighth or ninth except when adoption components alone were used (fourth) or when prices were adjusted for income (fifth), because its high income makes its brand prices look low relative to capacity. Spearman correlations with the headline were 0.95 or higher for every change in normalization, data source, or sample other than geometric aggregation (0.87), income-adjusted payment (0.82), and the entry of trial hosting or tax subsidies (0.87-0.92), and 0.53-0.72 for single-domain indices (Table 2). Substituting trial hosting for R&D input moved Japan, which hosts the fewest industry-sponsored trials per capita in the sample (3.4 starts per million per year, against 21.5 in Australia), from fifth to third. Dropping each country in turn left the top two and the bottom two unchanged and reordered the middle (Appendix Table S10).

The Monte Carlo analysis (Figure 1B, Table 2) confirmed a stable frame around a contested middle. South Korea and Australia ranked in the top three in 99% and 83% of specifications; Germany ranked in the bottom three in 88% and Switzerland in 65%. Canada ranked in the top three in 58% of specifications and Japan and Italy in the bottom three in 48% and 45%, with 5th-95th percentile rank intervals spanning six or seven positions. Without random source switching, Australia's top-three share rose to 100% and Italy's bottom-three share to 61% (Appendix Table S9).

## Internal structure

The five components were only moderately correlated among the nine countries (Spearman ρ from -0.25 to 0.73). Price and revenue contribution were correlated (0.63), as were availability and interval (0.73), but the payment and adoption domains were weakly related to each other (0.17-0.35) and R&D input was uncorrelated with price (0.02) and negatively correlated with revenue contribution (-0.25). The first principal component explained 53% of the variance and the second 22%, with the second separating payment from adoption; when the United States was included, the first component explained 76%, a one-dimensionality produced by the outlier. Effective importance was 0.65-0.66 for the adoption components, 0.51-0.55 for the payment components, and 0.24 for R&D input. Availability and interval were negatively associated with country size (ρ = -0.63 and -0.50 with log GDP), whereas brand price was not (0.23); residualizing all components on log GDP left the ranking unchanged.

## Comparison with single-metric measures

The headline index was weakly and imprecisely correlated with the Kolchinsky-Xie Freeriding Index across the seven overlapping countries (ρ = 0.25; Fisher 95% interval -0.62 to 0.84). Decomposition explains why (Figure 3): the payment sub-index reproduced the price-based measure (ρ = 0.89; interval 0.43 to 0.98), whereas the adoption sub-index was unrelated to it (ρ = -0.04). The two measures agree that Australia ranks high but disagree on Germany, which the price-based measure ranks close to France at the top and our index ranks lowest, and on Canada, which it ranks lowest and our index third. Correlations with ASPE's revenue-to-GDP and revenue-to-population ratios were 0.35 and 0.60 (nine countries), and with Frech et al's per-capita contribution -0.26 (six countries; Appendix Table S6), because those size-driven measures place Japan and Italy among the larger contributors whereas adoption and R&D indicators place them mid-table.

# Discussion

This study translates a theoretical concept, deviation from the dynamic-efficient payment rule by countries that rely on a larger market's R&D,[6] into a measurable, adjustable index. Three findings are robust to essentially any defensible analytic choice. First, South Korea and Australia combine low brand prices and revenue contributions with slow, partial reimbursement, and Germany and Switzerland contribute most among the nine countries, through fast, near-universal reimbursement and through research intensity respectively. Second, the middle of the ranking is contested: Canada pays more than any other non-US country for brand-name medicines but reimburses the fewest US-first drugs after the longest interval; Japan pays mid-range prices but hosts the fewest trials and reimburses just over half of US-first drugs; France, the United Kingdom, and Italy cannot be reliably ordered. Third, the payment and adoption channels are nearly orthogonal across countries, so a price-based measure and an adoption-based measure will rank the same countries differently.

## Why multidimensional measurement matters

The decomposition in Figure 3 is the study's most policy-relevant result. The Kolchinsky-Xie measure, which informed the "freeloading" framing of MFN policy, is a net brand-price calculation adjusted for income.[15] Our payment sub-index reproduces it almost exactly, which validates both; but the adoption sub-index carries information that the price measure does not contain. Germany ranks near the top of the price-based measure because German prices are low relative to German income, and lowest on our index because it makes 73% of US-first drugs reimbursable, after a mean interval of 1.1 years, and contributes 4.3% of OECD innovative-drug revenue. Canada is the mirror image: moderate on price, lowest on reimbursement of US-first drugs (36%) after a mean interval of more than three years.[16] From the innovator's perspective, non-reimbursement and delay reduce the expected return on which the dynamic-efficiency condition depends, and Philipson et al show that late adopters enter after US net prices have fallen.[16] Whether such delay is the payer's doing is a separate question: manufacturers file late or not at all in low-price and heavily referenced markets to protect prices elsewhere,[27-29,40,41] regulatory review adds months,[42] and in Japan listing follows approval within 60 to 90 days, so the interval there reflects sponsors' filing decisions. The adoption components describe the joint outcome of payer, regulator, and firm, which is what matters for innovators' returns, not HTA stringency alone. Revenue-share measures are complementary: they ask how much money flows from a country, which is largely a function of size,[8,14] whereas our index asks how a country compares with peers on each channel.

## Dynamic efficiency as a shared responsibility

Basu's result that a small country can deviate profitably describes an individually rational strategy; in the Olson-Zeckhauser equilibrium that Frech et al apply to pharmaceuticals, the public good is under-supplied and the largest contributor is exploited.[6-8] Four strands of evidence indicate that each country's own contribution matters for the pipeline. First, R&D responds to expected revenue in every market, not only the largest: a 1% increase in potential market size for a drug class is associated with a 4%-6% increase in new drug entry,[1] the elasticity of new chemical entities to global patent-life revenue is about 0.25, with roughly US$1.8 billion of additional revenue per additional entity,[2] demand expansions such as Medicare Part D raised pipeline entry,[3,43] and low-reimbursement segments such as US Medicaid show no detectable R&D response.[44] A recent review places the long-run elasticity of innovation to revenue between 0.25 and 1.5.[45] Second, paying less has costs for the payer: lower expected prices delay or foreclose launches,[27-29,40,41] and Japan illustrates the mechanism: drug loss was 52% for US-approved drugs from emerging biopharmaceutical companies in 2019-2023, compared with 19% in Europe.[46] Third, market expansion works in the other direction: China's national reimbursement reform was followed by an 86% relative increase in domestic drug trials, with dynamic gains estimated at three times the static access gains,[47] vaccine-use policies raised new-vaccine trials 2.5-fold,[5] and the Orphan Drug Act and the pneumococcal advance market commitment produced measurable entry.[48,49] Fourth, dependence on one payer is fragile. Under MFN, Medicare savings would exceed manufacturers' entire annual sales in the binding reference country for 73% of drugs,[50] launch benchmarks rest on small markets vulnerable to strategic delay,[51] early data show launches falling in reference markets,[51,52] European experience with reference pricing produced convergence and delayed access,[53] and a single international price at constant profits would raise prices outside the United States by 28% to more than 300%.[54]

The magnitude is not trivial. If the nine countries raised their innovative-drug revenue to parity with their GDP shares, OECD innovative-drug revenue would rise by about 16%, or 64% of their current combined contribution (Appendix Table S11); with an elasticity of 0.25-1.5,[2,45] that corresponds to roughly 4%-24% more new drugs per year, an illustrative rather than causal calculation. Contribution need not mean paying US prices: Germany and Switzerland reach low free-riding scores through fast listing and domestic R&D, and Australia's trial hosting, invisible to R&D statistics, is a further channel. Evidence that demand pull disproportionately funds incremental rather than novel products,[43,55] that opportunity-cost thresholds in publicly funded systems lie well below prevailing prices,[26,56] and that industry profitability and R&D spending do not by themselves justify prices[57,58] argues for value-based contributions paired with transparent HTA and push instruments, not unconditional price increases. The implication for payers and HTA bodies outside the United States is nonetheless direct: a system that relies on US revenue to sustain the medicines its own patients will need has a stake in raising its contribution through payment for value, faster and broader listing, and domestic R&D, and should expect entry consequences if it does not.

## Relevance to current policy

MFN implementation has made cross-country burden sharing a live negotiating variable. By September 2026, 26 manufacturers had signed MFN agreements covering most of the US branded market,[59] the United Kingdom had agreed to raise net prices for new medicines by 25% and its cost-effectiveness threshold to £25,000-35,000 per quality-adjusted life-year in exchange for tariff relief,[11,12] and Germany faced a Section 301 investigation.[13] A transparent index serves three purposes here. It locates each HTA system on each channel; the United Kingdom's threshold increase, for instance, addresses price but not the interval to reimbursement.[12,60] It gives governments negotiating with the United States a defensible alternative to price-only benchmarks, with the weights behind any claim made explicit in the dashboard. And it provides a reproducible baseline for tracking post-MFN change annually.

Japan illustrates the multidimensional view. Its brand price (22% of US) and revenue ratio (0.73) are mid-range, yet only 56% of US-first drugs were reimbursed by 2023 and it hosts the fewest industry-sponsored trials per capita in the sample. As of March 2023, 86 of 143 medicines approved in the United States or Europe in 2016-2020 but not in Japan had no Japanese development under way,[61,62] and the health ministry attributes sponsors' decisions not to file to pricing predictability as well as to regulatory requirements and the absence of emerging-company subsidiaries.[46,61] Drug loss thus enters the index through adoption even though Japan's payment components are mid-range.

## Limitations

Several limitations warrant caution. First, the price component uses manufacturer list prices; US rebates are larger than elsewhere, and RAND's 37.7% net-price adjustment narrows the all-drug US-to-comparator gap from 278% to 173%.[30] Applying it to both payment components changed no rank (S12), but rebates and clawbacks in comparator countries, which could not be quantified, would move individual countries. Second, the two adoption components derive from a single industry-compiled dataset restricted to US-first launches;[16] they may understate adoption where drugs launch first in Europe, count products rather than clinical value, so that a system declining low-benefit products is scored as free-riding,[63-65] exclude early-access and managed-access schemes, and reflect firms' launch strategies and regulatory timing as well as payer decisions. The European W.A.I.T. indicator, which starts at marketing authorization and thus excludes regulatory lag, is broadly concordant (rank correlation 0.70 for availability and for median time; Appendix Table S7), and substituting it for the five European countries left the ranking unchanged. Third, the interval is conditional on reimbursement; imputing the follow-up horizon to non-reimbursed drugs changed no rank (S14). Fourth, pharmaceutical R&D statistics are not harmonized: OECD values for France and Italy are about one third of association figures, the United Kingdom and Australia required national series, and trial R&D performed by contract research organizations is not captured; the component's effective importance is low (0.24) and its main effect is to separate Switzerland. Fifth, the United States is the reference point for three components and the index is relative to the nine-country sample; the France-Japan-United Kingdom-Italy block reorders when individual countries are removed. Sixth, the index is a cross-section drawn from sources with reference years between 2014-2019 (adoption cohort) and 2025 (revenue); it cannot capture price increases that MFN agreements may induce, and because firms respond to MFN by delaying launches, future adoption scores will partly reflect US policy and reference-pricing networks rather than reference-country behavior.[29,41] Seventh, cost-effectiveness thresholds are described but not scored (Appendix Table S5), because most systems use implicit or no thresholds and because price acceptance is a function of value delivered. Finally, with nine countries no inferential claims are possible; disease-area heterogeneity, patient affordability, and public research funding are not captured; and "free-riding" is a descriptive label for relative position, not a judgment about fair contribution, which depends on ability to pay, system design, and the value medicines deliver.[24-26,56]

## Future work

The pipeline is designed for annual updating. Priorities are decomposing the interval into sponsor filing, regulatory review, and payer decision; value-weighted availability; a disease-area breakdown; patient and clinician input to the weights; and extension to the full MFN reference basket.

# Conclusions

A five-component Free-Rider Index built from published data identifies a stable frame among nine high-income countries: South Korea and Australia consistently combine low payment with slow, partial adoption; Germany and Switzerland contribute through fast listing and research intensity; Canada, Japan, France, the United Kingdom, and Italy occupy a middle whose order depends on which channel is weighted. Because the payment and adoption channels are nearly independent across countries, debates that rely on price-only measures misidentify which channel differs and which lever matters. The evidence that R&D responds to expected revenue in every market, and that reliance on a single payer is now a demonstrated source of risk, implies that dynamic efficiency is a shared responsibility: each country's contribution to the medicines of the future is a policy choice with measurable consequences for its own patients.
